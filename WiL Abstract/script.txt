 SECTION 1 -- DERIVATIVES
=====================================================================

--- Running a regular expression -------------------------------------
Here's the basic question: we've got a pattern, we've got a word,
does the word match? There are two classic ways to answer that.

The textbook way is to compile the pattern into an automaton and run
the word through the machine, using something like Thompson, or
Glushkov, or the subset construction.

The other way, which is the one we take, is to never build a machine at
all. You just *differentiate* the pattern. And the key point is
that differentiating gives you the automaton anyway, without you ever
setting out to build one.


--- The derivative of a regular expression ---------------------------
So what's a derivative? The derivative of r with respect to a symbol a
is "what's left to match after we've read an a."

On languages it's straightforward: take every word in the language that
starts with a, and chop off that a. On expressions we compute it by
recursion on the syntax, and these are the rules..

Most of the rules are what we'd expect. Differentiating a symbol by
itself gives the empty word, since the symbol is fully consumed; and
differentiating it by any other symbol gives the empty set, since
nothing matches.


Little example at the bottom: differentiate "ab plus ac" by a, and we
get "b plus c", which is exactly the two ways to continue after an a.


--- Derivatives with respect to a word -------------------------------
We extend this from single symbols to whole words the obvious way:
differentiate by the first letter, then by the rest. Same recipe on
languages and on expressions.

And in the example, if we differentiate "ab plus ac" by the whole
word "ab", we land on epsilon, which is exactly the statement that
"ab" is in the language. So matching a word is just: differentiate by
it, and check whether the result contains epsilon (is nullable).

The theorem at the bottom makes the two worlds line up: differentiating
the expression and then reading off its language gives us the same
language as differentiating the language directly. So syntax and semantics
agree.


--- Regularity and finiteness ----------------------------------------
Two facts we lean on. First, derivatives keep us inside the regular
languages, so if we differentiate a regular language, it's still
regular.

Second: over a finite
alphabet a regular language has only *finitely many* distinct
derivatives. [i should probably talk more about finiteness here]


--- Canonicalization -------------------------------------------------
There's a catch, though. "Finitely many derivatives" is up to language
equality, but syntactically the expressions keep growing. We get
things like "a-star, times epsilon, plus empty-set" that *mean* a-star
but don't *look* like it.

So we normalize. Every expression gets rewritten to one canonical form,
using the standard laws for each operator: flatten sums, drop the
empty set, sort and dedupe; simplify products; collapse the degenerate
star cases. Two expressions are equivalent under these laws exactly when
their canonical forms are literally the same syntax tree.

Take the example: the expression on the left collapses to just
"a plus b plus a-star."

One caveat here. These laws are a finite set, but they do not
capture *full* semantic equivalence. In fact Salomaa, and later Kozen,
showed there is no finite purely equational axiomatization of
regular-expression equivalence at all, and the culprit is the star,
which can only ever be approximated by finitely many equations.


=====================================================================
 SECTION 2 -- AUTOMATA
=====================================================================

--- From derivatives to automata -------------------------------------
Reading a symbol is taking a derivative, and there
are only finitely many canonical derivatives, so let the canonical
derivatives *be* the states.

Start state is the expression itself, transitions are "derive and
canonicalize," and a state is accepting when it can match the empty
word. That's the whole DFA, and it accepts exactly the language of the
expression.

One remark worth making: if we could quotient by *full*
semantic equivalence we'd get the minimal automaton. Canonical forms
get us a finite one, usually minimal, though not always.


--- Constructing the automaton ---------------------------------------
Operationally it's just a graph search. Start at the canonical start
state, and for each symbol: derive, canonicalize, and look at what we
get. If it's a state we've already seen, just record the transition
and stop. If it's new, add it and recurse. When nothing new shows up,
we're done. That's the pseudocode on the right.


--- Example: the automaton of s-star --------------------------------
Let's run the pseudocode on an example. Our pattern is s-star, where s
is "a, or m, or r-then-i."

[1] We start at the start state, q0, the canonical form of s-star. The
algorithm now says: go through the alphabet one symbol at a time and
differentiate q0 by each. So we take the symbols in order, a, m, r, i.

[2] First symbol, a. Differentiating q0 by a, naively, gives this
unwieldy expression, namely "epsilon plus empty plus empty-times-i, all
times s-star." Left as-is, that would be a brand new state, in red.

[3] But we canonicalize, and it *folds right back into q0*, so a just
loops back to where we are. We move on to the next symbol, m, and it
behaves in exactly the same way, another self-loop on q0. This is the
crucial point: without canonicalization every step would spawn a fresh
state and the construction would never stop. 

[4] On to the next symbol, r. Differentiating q0 by r gives something
genuinely new, "i times s-star." Since this is a state we have not seen
before, the algorithm adds it as q1, records the edge from q0 to q1,
and then, importantly, starts over from q1. This is the recursion:
whenever we discover a new state, we pause where we are, go and explore
that new state fully, differentiating it by every symbol, before coming
back - so basically we are doing a dfs.

[5] So now we are working from q1, going through its symbols in turn.
Reading a, m, or r from q1 all fail, so we get the empty set, which is
another new state, the dead state q2. We add it and recurse once more,
into q2; but q2 differentiates to itself on every symbol, so it only
loops, and that branch closes right away.

[6] The last symbol left for q1 is i, which completes the r-i block and
lands back on q0, a state we already have, so we simply record the edge.
That was the final symbol for q1, so q1 is now fully explored, and we
climb back up to where we paused at q0.

[7] Back at q0, the one symbol we had not reached yet is i.
Differentiating q0 by i sends us straight to the dead state q2, which we
already have, since reading an i with no r before it fails. We record
that edge.

[8] Now every state has been differentiated by every symbol and nothing
new has appeared, so the construction halts. We mark the accepting
states, which here is just q0.

 SECTION 3 -- ROCQ
=====================================================================


--- Why formalize, and what is Rocq? ---------------------------------
This theory is easy to state and surprisingly easy to get subtly wrong,
whether it's the concatenation case, the star case, or the finiteness
argument. "It's obvious" and "I checked it on paper" are precisely
where mistakes hide. So we formalize it.

Rocq, which you may know as Coq, is an interactive proof assistant.
We state a theorem, we build a proof, and a tiny trusted kernel
verifies every step. Nothing is left on trust. It's the same tool that
checked the CompCert C compiler and the Four Colour Theorem.


--- Type theory and constructive logic -------------------------------
Rocq's foundation is a dependent type theory. By the
Curry-Howard correspondence, a proposition *is* a type and a proof *is*
a term of that type, so proving something means literally constructing
a term.

And because the logic is constructive, proofs carry computational
content: a proof that something exists actually provides the witness.
That will matter shortly, when I mention finiteness.


--- What a proof really is -------------------------------------------
Concretely, here's the semantic-syntactic correspondence, which says
"w is in the derivative of r by u exactly when u-followed-by-w is in
r." And here's the entire proof: three tactic lines. Induction on the
word, a rewrite, and some bookkeeping about concatenation.

 SECTION 4 -- FUZZY LAYER
=====================================================================

--- When exact matching is too strict --------------------------------
Everything so far hangs on one yes-or-no question: are these two
symbols the *exact same* symbol? And often that's too strict. Real text
is noisy: typos, lookalike characters, an "l" that is really a "1".
OCR and speech recognition don't hand us a clean symbol at all; they
hand us each symbol with a confidence, a best guess that could easily
have been a close neighbour, so insisting on exact equality throws that
information away. And in DNA, the letters are not really interchangeable
tokens: chemically some bases are much closer than others, so a near
match there is genuinely meaningful, not just a typo to tolerate.

So we'd like the pattern to keep matching when the input is merely
*close*. And the question we ask is minimal: keep the
crisp derivative theory *exactly* as it is, and change one single
thing, which is that matching two symbols now means matching up to
mu-similarity.


--- Two ways to fuzzify ----------------------------------------------
Fuzzy regular languages aren't new, and there are two routes. One puts
the fuzziness on *membership*, so a word belongs to a language to a
degree in the interval. The other one, puts it on
*similarity*, so membership stays yes-or-no, but the equality
underneath it goes graded.

We take the similarity route on purpose: closeness is a property of the
data itself, the letters, the bases, the sounds, rather than a number
attached to the language afterwards. We are simply softening the
equality of symbols.


--- Similarity relations on symbols ----------------------------------
So we grade how close two symbols are with a fuzzy relation R into the
unit interval. We ask for two levels of structure. Proximity:
reflexive and symmetric, meaning every symbol is fully like itself, and
closeness is mutual. Similarity: additionally a reverse triangle
inequality with respect to a T-norm.

A T-norm is just conjunction generalized to the interval; throughout,
we take Godel's minimum. And a *cut* value mu turns degrees back into
yes-or-no, so two symbols count as matching when their similarity is at
least mu.


--- From symbols to words and languages ------------------------------
Now we define similarity on words. Two words of the same length are
compared letter by letter: we take the similarity of each pair of
symbols in matching positions and combine them with the T-norm. Two
words of different lengths get similarity zero. And for languages we use
this two-sided inf-sup: every word on each side has to have a close
partner on the other. Both of these are again similarity relations.


--- Similarity on expressions ----------------------------------------
We also define similarity on expressions. Two expressions of the *same
shape* are compared by the similarity of the symbols in matching
positions. But notice this is syntax-sensitive: "a plus b" and "b plus
a" denote the same language, yet their expression-similarity can be
zero. So shape matters here, and we'll have to be careful about that.


--- Similarity on expressions: general T-norm ------------------------
For a general T-norm the same-shape formula is too generous, so we give
a proper inductive definition on the structure of restricted
expressions. Under Godel's minimum it agrees with the simple version,
so for the rest of the talk we can keep the simpler picture in mind.


--- Shape bounds meaning ---------------------------------------------
Here's how syntax and semantics relate in the fuzzy world. Syntactic
similarity is always a *lower bound* on the semantic one, and we
recover the true semantic value by ranging over all expressions with
the right languages, taking the most similar representatives.

One important caveat: intersection and complement break this bridge. So
all our fuzzy theorems live on the *restricted* fragment, so union,
concatenation, and star.


--- Fuzzy derivatives ------------------------------------------------
Now the derivative itself. A symbol counts as read when it's
mu-*similar* to the input, not only when it's equal. On languages,
that's the fuzzy language derivative. On expressions, the fuzzy
derivative is a *sum* of ordinary Brzozowski derivatives, taken over
all symbols and sub-expressions close enough to the ones we have.

And crucially, that sum is *finite*, because over a finite alphabet
only finitely many expressions are similar to a given one at all. So we
have not left the finite, regular setting, and fuzzy derivatives of
regular expressions remain regular.


--- Godel's min: fuzzy = crisp over a quotient -----------------------
And here's the result that ties the whole talk together. Under Godel's
minimum, the cut relation isn't just reflexive and symmetric, it's
also *transitive*. It's an equivalence relation. So we can bundle
mu-similar symbols into equivalence classes and form a *quotient
alphabet*.

And then a fuzzy derivative over the original alphabet is *exactly* an
ordinary, crisp derivative over the quotient alphabet. So the fuzziness
doesn't require any new theory, it just says "work up to the classes."


--- Everything carries over ------------------------------------------
And this is the essential consequence: because the fuzzy theory is just
the crisp theory read over the quotient alphabet, every earlier result
transfers without extra work. The syntax-semantics correspondence,
finiteness of derivatives, and DFA correctness all carry over, and the
quotient transition reads back as a fuzzy transition, holding to
degree mu. 


=====================================================================
 CONCLUSION
=====================================================================

--- Conclusion -------------------------------------------------------
So, to conclude. The classical derivative theory, all the way to DFA
correctness, is formalized in Rocq. We fuzzify through *similarity on
symbols* rather than membership degrees. And the quotient alphabet
reduces the fuzzy theory to the crisp one, so everything carries over.

What's next: formalizing the fuzzy layer in Rocq too; a properly
*constructive* finiteness result via normalized representatives, so
that Kuratowski-finiteness point, which matters because not all notions
of finiteness coincide constructively; and moving beyond Godel's
minimum to a general T-norm.
